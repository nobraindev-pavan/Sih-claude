# 06 · The ML layer — decision-relevant, and not circular

The concept note states the right principle — *"do not use AI merely as a label; the ML
layer should produce a decision-relevant quantity"* — and then proposes something that
violates it. This document fixes that.

## The trap

Generate synthetic data with a rule → train a model on it → the model learns your rule.
You have built an expensive identity function. A judge asks *"what does the model know that
your formula didn't?"* and there is no answer.

Two escapes, and we use both:

1. **Generate data from a different process than the model family.** Simulate failures from a
   Weibull hazard with confounders, unobserved variables and noise; fit gradient boosting.
   Now the model is genuinely estimating something it was not told, and reporting its
   calibration is a real result.
2. **Pick a target where the model's output is consumed by the optimizer.** Then "the ML
   changed the schedule" is demonstrable, not asserted.

## Model A (primary) — block duration and overrun

**Target:** `actual_duration_min` (regression) and `P(overrun)` (classification), from
`execution_log`.

**Why this is the right primary model:**

- **It is consumed.** `predicted_duration_min` is the `dur` parameter of every interval
  variable in the CP-SAT model (doc 5). Change the prediction, the schedule changes. The
  ML is load-bearing.
- **It is a real problem.** Blocks that overrun their sanctioned window are a genuine
  operational pain on IR — an overrun does not just delay the work, it cascades into train
  delays because the section is not returned on time.
- **It has an obvious real training set.** Every executed block already produces the
  actual start, actual end and completion status. Nothing confidential, nothing exotic —
  exactly the data an execution log holds. We can say precisely what real data would train it.
- **It gives us our best experiment.** Plan with nominal durations vs planned with predicted
  durations, then simulate execution with the *true* generating process and count overruns
  and cascaded train delay. That is a clean, honest, quantitative result about the value of
  the ML component.

**Features:** activity type · asset type and age · section and kilometre · single vs double
line · crew id, crew size and experience proxy · time of day and night flag · season and
monsoon flag · days since last maintenance on the asset · defect severity · number of other
tasks in the same block (congestion — a genuinely interesting learned effect) · block permit
set · access difficulty of the site · nominal duration.

**Model:** LightGBM or XGBoost regression for duration, plus a classifier for overrun.
Report **MAE and MAPE** for duration; **AUC, Brier score and a calibration curve** for
overrun. A calibration plot is the single most credible chart an ML student can show —
most SIH teams show accuracy and stop.

**Use quantiles, not just the mean.** Train a quantile regressor (LightGBM
`objective="quantile", alpha=0.8`) and feed the **P80 duration** to the optimizer, not the
mean. Planning to the mean means half your blocks overrun by construction. Explaining
*that* sentence to a judge demonstrates you understand what a prediction is for. Expose the
quantile as a UI slider: "plan conservatively / aggressively."

## Model B (secondary) — asset failure risk

**Target:** probability that an asset develops a reportable defect within the next 30 days.

**Frame it as survival/hazard, not naive classification.** Features: age, gross tonnage
carried (GMT), days since last maintenance, failure history, inspection findings, curvature
and gradient, rail weight, welds per km, drainage condition, season. Generate the truth with
a Weibull hazard whose shape depends on unobserved condition variables, so the model must
*estimate* rather than *recall*.

**Discipline that makes this defensible:**

- **Temporal split.** Train on months 1–9, test on 10–12. Never random-split time series;
  say so out loud, because random splitting is the most common error in student ML and a
  judge who catches it will discount everything else.
- **Calibration curve** on the test set. A well-calibrated 0.7 must mean 70%.
- **SHAP** for per-asset explanation: *"this OHE section scores 0.81 mainly because of 14
  months since last maintenance and 3 failures in 3 years."* Feeds directly into the
  explanation panel, and it is genuine explainability rather than a wrapper around a rule.
- **Report the baseline you beat.** Compare against "days since last maintenance alone."
  If the model doesn't beat that, say so and use the simple rule — and that honesty will
  score better than a fabricated improvement.

## Priority score — a rule, and we say it is a rule

The concept note's weighted sum is fine. Keep it, but be precise about what it is:

```
priority(t) = w1·criticality + w2·urgency(due_date)
            + w3·risk_score(Model B) + w4·asset_importance
            + w5·operational_impact + w6·overdue_factor
```

This is a **transparent policy, not a learned model**, and that is a virtue in a
safety-critical domain — a Sr. DEN can inspect and argue with the weights, which they
cannot do with a neural network. Say exactly that when presenting it. Derive default weights
by asking a domain contact to rank 20 sample tasks and fitting weights to match their
ranking; then you can say *"our weights were calibrated against a practitioner's ranking of
20 real-shaped cases,"* which no other team will be able to say.

## Delay propagation — simulation, not a GNN

Other teams are pitching GNN cascading-delay prediction. Don't. On synthetic data it cannot
learn more than the simulator you'd have to write anyway to generate the training labels, it
costs weeks, and a good judge will find it hollow.

Write ~30 lines instead: when a block delays a train by `d` minutes, propagate to trains
sharing subsequent sections subject to headway and platform capacity, decaying at each
station by available slack (the engineering allowance in the timetable). Deterministic,
inspectable, tunable, instant. It gives us the "trains affected downstream" number for the
disruption cost, and we can defend every line of it.

If a judge asks why not deep learning, the answer is prepared and strong: *"we have no real
operational history to train it on; a graph model fitted to our own simulator would only
recover our own assumptions with less transparency. In a safety-critical planning context
we chose the explainable mechanism. With three years of real COA data, a learned delay model
is the natural next step — and it plugs into the same interface."*

That answer wins the exchange. Rehearse it.
